About

I joined PolicyMe when the team was small enough that everyone did a bit of everything, and what I did was operations — running processes by hand, watching where they broke, and talking to customers when they did. It turned out to be the best possible training for product work.

Over seven years I moved from analyst to product manager and ended up owning our health and dental line end to end: a new product built with a distribution partner, then a direct-to-consumer launch on the same foundation, then renewals once the first cohort came due.

What I'm good at is taking a problem nobody has framed yet and making it concrete enough for a team to act on. Most of my work has lived where insurance rules, pricing logic, operational capacity and customer experience collide, and someone has to decide what actually gets built — and in what order.

Selected work

Four projects: a product built twice for two channels, a renewal cycle constrained by a date nobody could move, a self-service platform, and a year of funnel experiments.

      How I work

      • Start with the problem, not the feature.A feature request is usually a summary of something that went wrong upstream. It's worth finding out what.
      • Make ambiguity concrete.On a vague project the first deliverable is a written definition, not a design. Most disagreement turns out to be about wording nobody has pinned down yet.
      • Understand the operational reality behind the interface.Every screen has a person behind it processing exceptions. If I haven't sat with them, I don't understand the product yet.
      • Ship, then find out.A smaller thing in front of real customers beats a bigger thing that's still an argument. Most of what I've been sure about was half right.
      • Use frameworks as a starting point, not an answer.Scoring models are useful for making a decision discussable. They aren't a substitute for making it.
